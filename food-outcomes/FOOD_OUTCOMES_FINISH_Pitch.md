# Finishing cook-ahead dishes at the reheat: a proposal for the CONC kitchen

## What we are doing, and why

CONC feeds the people staying in our shelter. The kitchen puts out around 165 plates a meal, three meals a day, every day, on a four-week rotating menu, cooked across three sites while the main kitchen is being renovated, with a small crew.

At that volume, with that crew, we cannot cook everything fresh to order. So most of the heavier food is cooked in big batches a few days ahead, sealed and chilled, then sent to the serving site and reheated when it is time to eat. This is the only way the numbers work, and it is a normal, sound way to run a large kitchen.

The problem is what happens to a dish between the pot and the plate. Cooking ahead and reheating is hard on a few things:

- Cream and coconut sauces break, or go grainy and oily, after days in the fridge and a reheat.
- Fresh, bright flavors fade. The smell and the lift that make a curry taste just-made are mostly gone by the time it is reheated.
- A dish can reach the resident flat and a little broken, even though it tasted good the day it was cooked.

Here is why that is worth our attention. For someone who is unhoused or close to it, a hot meal that looks and tastes like someone cared is not a small thing. It is also practical: food that tastes and looks good gets eaten, and how much a person eats is a health question, not only a comfort one.

We have already done the work of getting the recipes themselves right, so a dish is genuinely what it says it is. This plan is the next step. It protects that good cooking so the dish still tastes and looks like itself after the cook-ahead process, instead of arriving as a tired version of what left the kitchen.

## The essential change

Right now we cook a dish all the way through, then chill it, then reheat it. The fragile parts that give a dish its life (the cream, the coconut, the fresh herbs, the squeeze of acid) go in early and do not survive the days in the fridge and the reheat.

The change is simple to say: build the sturdy base ahead, and add the fragile finishing parts at the reheat, right before the food goes out.

There are two versions of it, depending on the dish:

- For most dishes it is a small finishing touch at the reheat station. A squeeze of lime or lemon, a handful of fresh herbs, a spoon of spiced oil. Seconds of work. For a lot of these we are really just writing down what a good cook already does at the end.
- For the cream and coconut dishes it is a bit more. We cook the base without the cream or coconut, and stir it in at the reheat so it does not break. That is a real change to how the base is made, and we are saying so plainly.

## Who does the extra work

This matters, so it goes near the top. Almost all of the added work lands in one place.

- The cook at the reheat and plating end carries most of it. They do the stir-in or the finishing touch right before the food is sent out. For the small finishes this is seconds per batch.
- For the cream and coconut dishes, the cook making the base also changes one thing: they leave the cream or coconut out.
- Someone has to keep the finishing items stocked at the reheat site: cream and coconut milk, citrus, fresh herbs, a couple of spiced oils. Most of these are shared across many dishes, so it is a short standing list, not forty new things to buy.

Put plainly: for most dishes this is light and familiar work. For the cream and coconut dishes it is a genuine change, and those are also the dishes where it makes the biggest difference. We are not pretending the second kind is free.

## Where this stands today

The scheduling tool already prints the finishing step on the reheat card for eleven pilot dishes, so the instruction reaches the cook. That part is built and was checked carefully. It changed nothing about the menu, the cook days, the hold times, or the sites. It only added a line of guidance to the right cards.

What is not decided is the cooking itself. Nothing past those eleven goes into practice until the kitchen reviews the dishes below and signs off. The list that follows is a proposal, not a done deal.

## The dishes

We cook and reheat around forty dishes. (Across the four weeks there are 53 reheat batches, which come down to 43 distinct dishes once the repeats and the one shared sausage sauce are counted once.) They fall into four groups.

### Group 1: the cream and coconut dishes (the biggest wins)

These are the dishes where cooking the sauce ahead and reheating it does real damage. The dairy or coconut breaks and goes grainy or oily.

- The change: cook the base without the cream or coconut, then stir it in at the reheat, off the boil, so it stays smooth.
- The extra piece: a tub of cream or coconut milk held at the reheat site, one stir-in, a squeeze of acid, a salt check. Around a minute per batch.
- Who carries it: the reheat cook does the stir-in, and the base cook leaves the dairy or coconut out.
- Why it is worth it: a smooth, fresh-tasting sauce instead of a broken, flat one. This group is where the plan pays off most.

Dish by dish:

- **Butter Chicken, and the vegan Butter Chickpeas.** A tomato and cream curry with roasted chicken (chickpeas for the vegan). Now: cooked complete with the cream in, then bagged, chilled and reheated, which is what makes the sauce split. Finish: stir cream (coconut or cashew cream for the vegan) in off the boil, add crushed dried fenugreek, salt, a squeeze of lemon.
- **Beef Stroganoff, and the Vegan Mushroom Stroganoff.** Beef or mushroom in a sour-cream gravy over noodles. Now: cooked complete and reheated, and the sour cream curdles. Finish: stir the sour cream (vegan sour cream) in off the boil, a splash of lemon or vinegar, fresh dill.
- **Beef Massaman, and the Vegan Massaman.** A mild Thai coconut curry with potato. Now: cooked complete and reheated, and the coconut splits. Finish: stir coconut milk in over the last few minutes, off a hard boil, then brighten with lime and salt. Fish sauce is optional, and if it is used it adds fish, which has to go on the allergen list.
- **Green Curry (vegan).** A Thai green coconut curry. Now: reheated, coconut splits. Finish: coconut milk in late, off the boil, then lime and Thai basil.
- **White Chili pasta (chicken, and the vegan chickpea version).** A creamy white chili baked with pasta. Now: reheated, the cream sauce breaks. Finish: stir cream (vegan cream) in off the boil, then lime and cilantro.
- **Mashed Potato (a side).** Now: cooked ahead and reheated, it stiffens and dries out. This one does not split, it just goes stodgy. Finish: beat warm butter and a little milk back in at the reheat to loosen and enrich it.

### Group 2: the dishes that just need brightening

These hold and reheat fine on their own. What they lose is the fresh top note. A small finish at the reheat brings it back.

- The change: add a fresh finishing touch at the reheat station, right before serving.
- The extra piece: seconds of work, plus a few fresh items kept on hand (citrus, herbs, fresh chili, a spiced oil).
- Who carries it: the reheat cook. For most of these it is just formalizing what a careful cook already does at the end.
- Why it is worth it: it turns a flat reheated stew into one that tastes freshly made.

Dish by dish, with what it is and the finish:

- **Caribbean Chicken Curry, and the Chickpea version.** A thyme and scotch-bonnet curry. Finish: fresh thyme, a little scotch bonnet, a squeeze of lime.
- **Gomen Besiga, and the Vegan Gomen Besiga (on the menu as Vegetarian Collard Stew).** An Ethiopian collard and beef stew, mushroom for the vegan. Finish: a spoon of warm niter kibbeh, the spiced clarified butter, stirred through (a spiced oil for the vegan).
- **Sausage and Greens Pasta (regular, vegan, and the halal version listed as Halal Chicken and Vegetable Pasta).** Sausage, greens and pasta in one sauce. Finish: fresh basil or parsley and a hit of lemon.
- **Beef Stew.** A plain braise. Finish: fresh parsley and thyme, and a splash of red-wine vinegar.
- **Vindaloo (pork, chicken, and vegan).** A hot and sour Goan curry. Finish: a fresh tarka, which is whole spices and garlic bloomed in hot oil, poured over, plus a splash of vinegar.
- **Adobo (pork, and the halal chicken).** A Filipino soy and vinegar braise. Finish: a splash of fresh vinegar, a check on the pepper and bay, and fresh scallion.
- **Griot (pork, and the halal chicken).** Haitian braised pork. The braise reheats fine, the frying happens fresh. Finish: fresh lime and pikliz, or fresh chili and scallion.
- **Carnitas (pork, halal chicken, and vegan).** Braised, citrus-heavy Mexican pork. Finish: fresh lime, cilantro, and a little orange or citrus.
- **Chakalaka.** A South African spiced vegetable relish. Finish: fresh chili and a squeeze of lemon.
- **Chickpea Shakshuka.** Chickpeas in a spiced tomato sauce. Finish: fresh parsley or cilantro, and a drizzle of olive oil.
- **Eggplant and Cassava Stew.** A Caribbean vegetable stew. Finish: fresh herb, lime, and a little scotch bonnet.
- **Vegan Chili.** A bean chili. Finish: fresh lime, cilantro, a little raw diced onion, and a bloom of cumin.
- **West African Peanut Stew (regular and vegan).** A groundnut stew. Finish: fresh lime, cilantro, a little fresh chili. This dish contains peanut, so confirm the allergen list says so.

### Group 3: dishes to reconsider

These three are cooked ahead and reheated today, but they are the kind of food we normally cook fresh on the day, the roasted and grilled things. A finish can help, but the better question is whether they should be cooked ahead at all.

- **Tandoori (pork, and the halal chicken).** The whole point of tandoori is the char from high heat. Reheating it from chilled loses that. The real question is whether to cook it fresh on service day. If it stays cook-ahead, a finish of fresh lemon, chaat masala and cilantro helps a little.
- **Caribbean Chicken Drumsticks.** Roasted seasoned drumsticks. The skin will not re-crisp from chilled. Same question, and worth noting that we already cook most chicken legs fresh, so this one is the odd one out.
- **Baked Tomato Tofu.** Baked tofu in tomato. The texture softens on reheat. Same question: this is the kind of thing we usually cook fresh.

Who carries this one: nobody extra. If anything it is less work, because cooking fresh on the day removes the cook-ahead and reheat steps. The trade is kitchen time on the service day. That is a scheduling call, not a finishing question.

### Group 4: reheated dishes that do not need a finish

A handful of reheated items are not sauced braises, so the finishing idea does not apply:

- The CONC Salad protein mixes (beef and vegan) are cooked, then assembled cold into a salad. Their freshness comes from the cold assembly.
- The Philly melts (cheese, and vegan mushroom) are hot sandwich fillings. Their quality is a plate-up question for later.
- The Philly Peppers and Onions side could take a splash of vinegar, nothing more.
- The Nacho mixes (beef and vegan) are toppings. A little fresh cilantro and lime would not hurt, but it is optional.

## What we need from the kitchen

This is a proposal. To put it into practice we need your read on a few things:

- Amounts. None of the finishes above give quantities. You set the real ratios, like how much cream per pan and how much niter kibbeh per batch.
- Ingredients on hand. Are the finishing items available for the right days: dried fenugreek, niter kibbeh and a vegan spiced oil, fresh scotch bonnet, fresh dill, pikliz.
- The cream and coconut question, per dish. Do you already hold the cream or coconut back, in which case this just writes it down, or is it cooked into the batch today, in which case it is a real change.
- The three reconsider dishes. Keep cooking them ahead, or move them to fresh on the day.
- Allergens, before anything goes live. Any finish that adds an allergen, like fish sauce adding fish, has to be added to the allergen list. We should also confirm the list is already correct for the dishes we are touching: the peanut stew should say peanut, and the cream dishes should say milk.
- Where to start. The cream and coconut group is the biggest win, so it is the natural place to begin.

## What this is not

- It is not a change to the menu, to what the recipes are, to the cook days, the hold times, or the sites.
- It is not the plate-by-plate garnishing at serving, the fresh herb or the crunch on top. That is a later step that pairs with a change to which sites are active.
- Past the eleven pilot dishes, nothing is in practice until the kitchen signs off on the dishes above.

## The short version

We cook ahead because we have to. This plan keeps the fragile, flavor-giving parts of a dish out of the cook-ahead and adds them back at the reheat, right before serving. For most dishes that is a few seconds and a fresh touch the cooks largely do already. For the cream and coconut dishes it is a real but small change to how the base is cooked, and it is where the food improves the most. The work lands mainly on the reheat cook. The payoff is food that reaches our residents tasting and looking freshly made.
